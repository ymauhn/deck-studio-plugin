Millions of sequences, few known functions

  • Proteins take part in almost every cellular process
  • Sequencing is fast; experimental characterization is slow and expensive
  • Few records carry experimentally supported annotations
  • Automated prediction tries to close the gap (CAFA benchmark)

Proteins catalyze reactions, signal, transport and give structure.

Sequence databases grow far faster than experimental annotation, so most proteins have no experimentally supported function.

CAFA measures automated predictors on proteins whose function was not yet public.

Function as a hierarchy of terms

  • Three ontologies: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), Molecular Function (MF)
  • Each is a directed acyclic graph of terms (is_a, part_of)
  • True Path Rule: a term implies all its ancestors
  • Task: hierarchical multi-label classification

A protein carries many terms at different levels of specificity. If a specific term is annotated, every ancestor must be too.

Predictions are made hierarchy-consistent before any metric is computed.

Function also depends on context

  • Proteins act in complexes and pathways
  • PPI networks: nodes are proteins, edges are associations
  • Guilt by association: connected proteins tend to share functions
  • But STRING mixes evidence of uneven reliability, and about one third of proteins have no edge

Sequence-derived features are strong, but part of a protein's function is set by its cellular context.

STRING aggregates experiments, curated databases, co-expression, genomic context and text mining into one confidence score.

Coverage is uneven, which becomes the central limitation of a network-only model.

Seven research questions

RQ1How do different representations of the STRING PPI network data impact the quality of functional prediction across the three GO domains?
RQ2To what extent does fixed propagation of ProtT5 embeddings over the STRING network improve upon the network-only representation, and how does this effect vary across BP, MF, and CC?
RQ3How do different strategies, ranging from simple graph embeddings to fixed non-parametric propagation and GNN approaches, compare under a common experimental protocol and across vector- and image-based classification architectures?
RQ4Do early- and late-fusion ensembles improve the primary evaluation metric over their individual fixed-propagation and GNN components?
RQ5How do the propagation depth and the retention coefficient affect predictive performance, and at what point does additional propagation begin to dilute the protein-specific signal?
RQ6How does the proposed approach compare against established state-of-the-art network-driven algorithms when all methods are evaluated using the same data splits and transductive graph setting?
RQ7Can the proposed methodology generalize effectively when applied directly to global, aggregated-evidence networks, without the need to partition the graph by specific species or evidence channels?

The questions as worded in Section 1.3. RQ7's parenthesis: aggregated-evidence networks combine multiple interaction sources and cross-species data into a single unified topology. Each question is answered on the conclusions slide.
